13 brands. One system. Four weeks.

Dalhousie University needed a unified hospitality identity that could span 5 dining halls and 8+ retail cafes across two campuses. Each location needed its own personality. All of them needed to feel like they belonged together.

This was part of a $180M RFP response. The kind of brand architecture that takes an agency team 3-4 months. I did it solo in four weeks because the timeline didn't care about what's typical.

Level 1
Dalhousie Hospitality
Master brand. Umbrella identity anchoring every dining experience to the university's 200-year heritage.
Level 2
Risley, Howe, Shireff, O'Brien, Jenkins
Dining halls. Each with a unique icon and identity reflecting its location and community. Destinations, not cafeterias.
Level 3
Ground Zero, Green Cafe, Boba Tea House, Brief Break, Frederic's, Cyber Cafe +
Retail cafes. Specialty concepts with distinct personalities. From Starbucks partnerships to boba kiosks, all part of the family.

Started with the eagle.

Before designing anything, I deconstructed Dalhousie's existing visual identity. The crest, the typography, the black and gold palette. I pulled an abstracted beak form from the eagle crest and made it a recurring motif throughout the system. Every sub-brand traces back to the university's DNA.

Testing every direction.

Laurels, shields, typographic systems, icon grids. I explored how each visual language would scale across 13 locations before committing. The laurel won because it connects naturally to academic tradition and the idea of harvest and gathering.

Concept 01
The Circle System
Concept 02
The Woodblock System

Two directions. Same rigor.

The Circle System uses a laurel-framed monogram as the master brand, with each dining hall getting a unique icon inside a gold ring. Cafes extend the system through color-coded circles for wayfinding.

The Woodblock System takes a bolder graphic approach. Shield-based architecture, stronger typographic presence, and a direct tie back to Dalhousie's crest through an abstracted eagle head.

Both were fully developed. Not sketches. Not mood boards. Complete systems with environmental mockups, ready for stakeholder review.

From dining halls to boba kiosks.

The system had to flex from a 200-seat residence hall to a playful food court kiosk. Boba Tea House got a custom mascot, illustrated menu items, branded roll-up banners, and environmental graphics.

Same brand architecture. Completely different personality. That's what a real system does.

Every location, production-ready.

Environmental mockups weren't conceptual fluff. Every cafe got full signage specs, menu board templates, and material callouts that a fabricator could work from without a follow-up call.

The goal was never to impress a committee. It was to hand over files that could be built.
